The dark point in the title is a live Schwarzschild black hole. Its gravity bends the light of the letters and of the Milky Way behind them.

The name

In Norse cosmology, Ginnungagap is the void that existed before anything else. Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda (c. 1220) places it between Niflheim, a world of ice and mist in the north, and Muspelheim, a world of fire in the south.

Rime from the north crept into the gap. Where it met the heat of the south it thawed, and from the dripping meltwater came Ymir, the first being. The gods later built the world from his body and set it in the middle of the gap.

The older poem Völuspá recalls that moment in one line: gap var ginnunga, en gras hvergi (“there was a yawning gap, and grass nowhere”). The meaning of ginnunga is still debated. It may come from a root meaning “to gape”, from one meaning “to deceive”, or from the prefix ginn-, which marks things as vast and charged with power.

The project

Ginnungagap is a laboratory for the physics of extreme places, built so you can see what the equations predict instead of only reading them.

Every image is computed from physical law. Light is traced along geodesics of curved spacetime, gas glows at the temperature the theory assigns it, and the numbers beside each view come from the same equations that draw it.

The first module is a Schwarzschild black hole with a thin accretion disk. You can change its mass, move around it, and switch individual relativistic effects on and off to see how each one shapes the picture.

The second puts Earth and the Moon where they are right now, at true scale, and draws the curvature of spacetime around them. Add planets or spacecraft, and compare how fast clocks tick in different places.

The name fits. Just outside the horizon, gas in the disk reaches millions of kelvin, while the horizon itself radiates at about 60 billionths of a kelvin for a black hole of one solar mass. Fire and ice, at the edge of a void.

Spacetime grid

The sheet dips where clocks run slow. Its depth at each point is the gravitational potential Φ, the part of spacetime’s curvature that steers slow-moving bodies like the Moon: gtt = −(1 + 2Φ/c²).

The real dip is tiny. At Earth’s surface clocks lose 0.7 parts per billion, so the depth is scaled up until you can see it. Depth is measured from the sheet’s outer edge, since only differences in potential matter. Wells narrower than the grid spacing are smoothed to that spacing; zoom in to see their true shape.

Zoom far out and the sheet turns into a saddle, dipping toward and away from the Sun. That is the Sun’s tide, the same stretching that raises ocean tides. Beyond about 1.5 million km it outweighs Earth’s own pull, which is why nothing can orbit Earth farther out than that.

At ×1 the deepest well fills about a third of the view. Move right for deeper, steeper wells; double-click the slider to return to ×1.

Two perfect clocks start together. Gravity and speed make them tick at slightly different rates, and the gap grows as simulated time runs.

Why they differ

Each bar is how much slower a clock ticks than one at rest far away. Deeper in a gravity well is slower, and so is faster motion.
